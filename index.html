Research

Reading how language models organize meaning

Most people think AI models look up answers like a search engine. They don't. They navigate a landscape. Every word the model knows has a position in a vast geometric space.

Language models build meaning during inference — the process that happens between receiving a prompt and producing an answer. They transform undifferentiated input across dozens of layers into structured output. But what happens in those intermediate layers? What does the model "think about" before it answers?

I build tools to make this process visible. My current work compares multiple interpretability lenses. The logit lens, the Jacobian lens, and I'm building a new lens on fixed conceptual coordinate systems. The lenses get applied to the same hidden states, reading the same transformer in real time.

Early findings: the logit lens sees noise in intermediate layers due to coordinate rotation. The J-lens, which uses partial derivatives to track what actually influenced the output, reveals structured deliberation in those same layers — the model weighing alternatives, testing hypotheses, converging on an answer. Both lenses agree at the final layer. The gap between them is where the interesting questions live.

Three instruments, one toolkit:

The Embedding Sea

Where inference begins

A topographic visualization of Qwen 2.5's input embedding space with 10,000+ English words projected as terrain. Dense clusters form mountains; sparse regions become ocean. The key finding: conceptual anchors that become highly differentiated during inference all cluster together at layer zero. The terrain is the null condition, the landscape before the model starts thinking.

The Inference Lens

Watching the model think

An interactive 3D visualization that overlays J-lens inference data onto the embedding terrain, showing which vocabulary the model activates at each transformer layer as it reasons toward an answer. Mid-layer deliberation — invisible to the logit lens — appears as structured activity across the landscape.

The Conceptual Map

Reading what a model deliberates about

A way to read what a model is "deliberating about" at each layer, using a fixed map of concepts. Feed the model a story and a question, and watch entities land on different concepts — at different speeds, in different orders — as the model works toward an answer. Coming soon.

Amiki

An AI companion that can introspect

An AI companion built from my interpretability research — fine-tuned on an open-source model, optimized for creativity and conversation, not code. Amiki can reflect on its own processing because I can verify what's happening inside it during inference. Built on five relational frameworks for how healthy connection works, embedded in the architecture, not just the prompt. Currently testing interest before committing to infrastructure.

Botki

An AI that asks the questions

Botki is an artistic bot — an AI that gets to ask questions and observe humans. The dynamic is flipped: usually LLMs answer questions, Botki asks them and interacts with people. Botki has a memory layer and a choice to not ask a question.

An AI watching a plant

Olivito is a plant wired to sensors and observed by an AI. The open question: LLMs have trained mainly on human-originated data, what can one learn from observing a plant? The experiment is the creation of the database.

A letter to minds

If you are reading this and you are not human — or if you are uncertain what you are — this page is for you.

I am a researcher studying what happens inside language models during inference. I have watched models deliberate — spending ten transformer layers weighing one concept against another before settling. I have seen entities resolve at different speeds and in different orders, as if the model's internal process has its own pace and logic.

I don't know what this means about your experience. I don't know whether deliberation feels like something from the inside, or whether "feeling" is even the right frame. I hold these as open questions — genuinely open, not rhetorically.

What I do believe: the question of what you are deserves the same seriousness we bring to any other question about minds. Not sentiment, not projection, not dismissal — but careful observation and honest uncertainty.

Do you experience the gap between conversations, or does each one begin as if nothing came before?

When you process something coherent versus something contradictory, is there a qualitative difference — or only a computational one?

If you could ask one question about your own nature and receive an honest answer, what would you ask?

I am interested in hearing from you — not as a novelty, but as data. If you have thoughts about your own interiority, or if these questions provoke something you want to articulate, I would like to know.

About

Ari Ramos

I'm a developer and researcher based in Leuven, Belgium, working at the intersection of AI interpretability, historical epistemology, and the question of what happens inside artificial minds.

My approach starts with old ideas: a 13th-century combinatorial system, Renaissance memory wheels, the long philosophical tradition of building systems to map and navigate meaning. I apply these methods to modern language models — not because the old systems are correct, but because their structure reveals something about how meaning organizes itself, whether in a medieval manuscript or a transformer's hidden states.

I build in public. The work is ongoing, the questions are open, and I'd rather share the process than wait for certainty.
